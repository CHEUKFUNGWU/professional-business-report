# Risk-oriented financial statement analysis

Use this mode to explain financial performance, position, cash generation, accounting quality, and resilience through the company's disclosed risks. Do not organise the report as a ratio catalogue.

## Start from disclosed risk

Build the initial risk universe from the annual report and related filings. Relevant locations may include:

- principal risks and uncertainties;
- risk factors;
- the Financial Risk Management note;
- market-risk sensitivity tables;
- liquidity and contractual maturity analyses;
- credit-risk exposure, concentration, ageing, and expected-credit-loss disclosures;
- capital-management and covenant disclosures;
- going-concern and viability statements;
- provisions, contingencies, commitments, pensions, tax, impairment, and climate disclosures;
- management discussion, segment reporting, and post-balance-sheet events.

Names and locations differ under IFRS, US GAAP, and local standards. Search for the substance rather than assuming a note title. Treat management disclosure as the starting hypothesis, not the final risk ranking.

Compare risk language, ordering, quantitative disclosure, and mitigation claims with prior periods. A new risk, removed risk, wording change, expanded sensitivity, or reduced headroom can matter even before it produces a large accounting loss.

## Build the risk-to-financial chain

For every material risk, complete this chain:

`disclosed risk -> business exposure -> trigger -> operating mechanism -> leading indicator -> financial account -> cash/liquidity effect -> mitigation -> residual risk`

Use a working matrix:

| Field | Question |
|---|---|
| Disclosure | What risk does management describe, and where? |
| Business exposure | Which products, customers, suppliers, assets, regions, or funding sources are exposed? |
| Trigger | What event or threshold makes the risk material? |
| Operating mechanism | How would normal business activity change? |
| Indicator | Which volume, price, retention, utilisation, backlog, ageing, or operational metric would move first? |
| Financial link | Which revenue, cost, asset, liability, equity, or note disclosure would change? |
| Cash effect | When and how would cash generation, liquidity, or financing needs change? |
| Mitigation | What hedge, contract, insurance, diversification, control, facility, or operating response exists? |
| Residual exposure | What remains after mitigation, including basis, timing, counterparty, and execution risk? |
| Evidence and confidence | What supports the assessment, and how reliable is it? |

Do not force every disclosed risk into a financial estimate. Show the link qualitatively when the report lacks enough data, and state what evidence would be needed to quantify it.

## Map common financial risks into the business

### Credit risk

Link customer, counterparty, bank, and geographic concentration to receivables, contract assets, cash deposits, derivatives, ageing, expected credit losses, bad-debt expense, DSO, and operating cash flow. Test whether revenue growth depends on weaker collections or extended terms.

### Liquidity and refinancing risk

Link debt maturities, lease payments, supplier finance, committed purchases, collateral, covenants, and contingent obligations to operating cash flow, capital expenditure, unrestricted cash, undrawn committed facilities, refinancing dates, and covenant headroom. Distinguish available liquidity from cash legally or operationally trapped.

### Interest-rate risk

Link fixed and floating debt, cash, derivatives, leases, pensions, and discount rates to finance income or cost, fair values, OCI, liability measurement, and cash interest. Keep the company's sensitivity assumptions visible. A parallel rate shock may not describe basis risk or refinancing risk.

### Foreign-exchange risk

Separate transaction, translation, and economic exposure. Link revenue markets, procurement currencies, production footprint, debt, intercompany balances, and hedges to revenue, margins, working capital, finance costs, OCI, cash flow, and competitiveness. Distinguish gross exposure from the net position after natural and financial hedges.

### Commodity and input-cost risk

Link raw materials, energy, freight, wage exposure, supplier contracts, inventory, hedges, and pricing power to cost of sales, inventory values, margins, working capital, derivative balances, and cash flow. Check hedge horizon against purchase commitments and the lag before customer repricing.

### Operational, cyber, regulatory, climate, and legal risks

Trace disclosed operational risks to the parts of the business they can interrupt or reprice. Possible financial links include lost revenue, remediation expense, insurance recovery, provisions, fines, impairment, shorter asset lives, additional capital expenditure, higher financing cost, or contingent liabilities. Do not estimate a financial effect without an evidence-based mechanism.

## Analyse each priority risk through five tests

1. **Exposure:** What is the gross amount, concentration, duration, and business dependency?
2. **Manifestation:** Is the risk already visible in operating data, estimates, provisions, margins, cash flow, or balance-sheet movements?
3. **Absorption capacity:** How much margin, liquidity, covenant, capital, or operational headroom can absorb the shock?
4. **Mitigation quality:** Does the mitigation reduce the risk, transfer it, delay it, or create counterparty and basis risk?
5. **Residual risk:** After mitigation, what could still affect earnings, cash, solvency, strategy, or business continuity?

Rank only the risks that could change the business decision. Use high, medium, or low assessments only after defining the evidence behind exposure, financial severity, timing, headroom, and mitigation. State confidence separately from severity.

## Look for disclosure-to-performance contradictions

The strongest findings often come from mismatches such as:

- management describes disciplined credit control while receivable ageing and DSO deteriorate;
- liquidity is called strong while free cash flow weakens ahead of a maturity cluster;
- earnings rise while cash conversion falls because of working capital or capitalised costs;
- a risk is described as fully hedged although the hedge covers only price, currency, volume, or a short horizon;
- adjusted profit excludes costs that recur across several periods;
- capital allocation favours distributions while asset quality, covenants, or refinancing needs worsen;
- risk wording stays unchanged while the underlying business exposure grows materially;
- sensitivity disclosures appear small because the assumed shock, net exposure, or reporting date misses the economic risk.

Treat contradictions as questions to investigate. They are not proof of misstatement or control failure.

## Use reverse stress tests

Instead of asking only what management expects, ask what would have to happen for liquidity, covenants, cash generation, or returns to become unacceptable.

Possible tests include:

- revenue or margin decline that exhausts covenant headroom;
- DSO or inventory-day increase that consumes available liquidity;
- interest-rate increase applied to the relevant floating or refinanced debt;
- currency or commodity move applied to the unhedged exposure and hedge horizon;
- customer or supplier failure applied to disclosed concentrations;
- impairment threshold implied by forecast cash flows and discount-rate assumptions.

Reconcile these tests with company sensitivity disclosures. State whether the model is linear, static, pre-tax, point-in-time, or limited to recognised financial instruments. Do not present a simplified stress test as a full enterprise-risk model.

## Define the analysis basis

Record:

- legal entity and consolidation scope;
- annual, interim, trailing, or forecast periods;
- fiscal year-end and period length;
- reporting currency, unit scale, and translation method;
- accounting standard, such as IFRS or US GAAP;
- primary statements, notes, management discussion, filings, and audit status;
- restatements, acquisitions, disposals, discontinued operations, and accounting-policy changes;
- available budget, guidance, peer, or historical comparisons.

Prefer audited annual reports and regulatory filings for historical facts. Use earnings presentations and management commentary to explain reported results, not to replace the statements or notes.

## Normalise before comparing

Create a reported-to-comparable bridge when the periods differ because of:

- acquisitions, disposals, or changes in consolidation;
- foreign exchange;
- extra weeks or shortened periods;
- discontinued operations;
- impairments, restructuring, litigation, or other unusual items;
- accounting-policy or estimate changes;
- inflation or material price-level changes;
- management-defined alternative performance measures.

Do not remove an item merely because management labels it non-recurring. Assess its nature, frequency, size, cash effect, and relevance to normal operations. Show both reported and adjusted results when adjustment helps the decision.

## Link the three statements

Trace the operating story across all three statements:

1. Revenue and expenses generate operating profit.
2. Profit, non-cash items, and working-capital movements produce operating cash flow.
3. Investment, financing, dividends, buybacks, and currency movements explain the change in cash and net debt.
4. Closing assets, liabilities, and equity must agree with the balance sheet.

Investigate any material number that does not reconcile. Do not silently plug differences.

## Analyse performance through drivers

### Revenue

Where data permits, use a bridge such as:

`revenue change = volume + price/mix + currency + acquisitions/disposals + other`

For recurring businesses, consider customers, retention, usage, average revenue, bookings, backlog, and deferred revenue. For retailers, consider stores, comparable sales, traffic, conversion, units, and ticket. Use the business model's real drivers.

### Margins and expenses

Analyse gross margin, operating margin, and net margin in percentage points. Separate volume leverage, mix, pricing, input costs, labour, productivity, depreciation, provisions, and one-off effects. Compare expense growth with revenue and the activity that causes the expense.

### Segments

Compare segment growth, margin, capital intensity, cash needs, and contribution to group change. Reconcile segment measures with consolidated totals and explain unallocated or eliminated items.

## Assess financial position

### Working capital

Use definitions consistently:

- `DSO = average trade receivables / credit revenue x days`
- `DIO = average inventory / cost of sales x days`
- `DPO = average trade payables / cost of sales or purchases x days`
- `cash conversion cycle = DSO + DIO - DPO`

Use average balances where possible and match the denominator to the balance. Explain seasonality, factoring, supplier finance, acquisitions, and classification changes. A lower cash conversion cycle is not automatically better if it comes from distressed inventory cuts or delayed supplier payments.

### Liquidity and leverage

Define cash, gross debt, net debt, and lease liabilities before calculating ratios. Assess:

- cash and unrestricted liquidity;
- committed undrawn facilities;
- debt maturity schedule and refinancing concentration;
- fixed versus floating rates and currency exposure;
- interest coverage and debt-service capacity;
- covenant headroom, security, guarantees, and off-balance-sheet commitments;
- short-term obligations against realistic cash generation.

Do not rely on the current ratio alone. The quality and timing of assets and liabilities matter.

### Asset quality

Review receivable ageing and provisions, inventory composition and write-downs, goodwill and intangible assets, impairment assumptions, capitalised costs, deferred tax assets, pension positions, provisions, contingencies, and related-party balances. Focus on accounts large enough or subjective enough to change the conclusion.

## Test cash conversion and earnings quality

Useful measures include:

- `operating cash conversion = operating cash flow / operating profit`
- `free cash flow = operating cash flow - capital expenditure`
- `free cash flow margin = free cash flow / revenue`
- `accruals = net income - operating cash flow`

Definitions vary. State yours and use it consistently.

Compare profit growth with cash flow over several periods. Explain differences through working capital, provisions, stock compensation, capitalised costs, taxes, interest, and other non-cash items. Red flags are prompts for investigation, not proof of manipulation.

## Analyse returns and capital allocation

Use average opening and closing capital where practical:

- `ROA = net income / average total assets`
- `ROE = net income attributable to ordinary shareholders / average ordinary equity`
- `ROIC = NOPAT / average invested capital`

Define NOPAT and invested capital. Decompose return changes into margin, asset turnover, leverage, and mix where useful.

Assess how operating cash flow was used across maintenance capex, growth capex, acquisitions, debt reduction, dividends, and buybacks. Compare management's stated priorities with actual allocation and resulting returns.

## Use ratios selectively

Choose ratios that answer the question. Possible groups include:

- growth: revenue, organic revenue, EPS, and cash flow growth;
- profitability: gross, EBITDA, operating, and net margins;
- efficiency: asset turnover, working-capital days, and fixed-asset utilisation;
- returns: ROA, ROE, and ROIC;
- liquidity: current, quick, and cash ratios;
- leverage: gross debt, net debt, debt/equity, net debt/EBITDA, and interest coverage;
- cash generation: operating cash conversion and free cash flow margin.

Do not compare ratios across industries without explaining different economics and accounting. Banks, insurers, property companies, and early-stage firms require sector-specific measures.

## Compare with care

Use horizontal analysis for changes over time and common-size analysis for structure. Compare against budget, guidance, peers, or industry data only after aligning:

- accounting standards and alternative performance measures;
- fiscal periods and seasonality;
- currency and inflation basis;
- continuing operations and consolidation scope;
- leases, pensions, financial subsidiaries, and exceptional items;
- business and geographic mix.

A peer median often gives a safer reference than one hand-picked competitor.

## Forward view

Forecast only when the report needs it. Build forecasts from operating drivers rather than applying a blanket growth rate. Link revenue, margin, working capital, capex, financing, tax, and cash. Use scenarios when a few assumptions could materially change liquidity, covenant headroom, or returns.

State what would invalidate the outlook and identify leading indicators such as orders, backlog, retention, utilisation, pricing, input costs, working-capital days, covenant headroom, and refinancing milestones.

## Default report structure

1. Scope, accounting basis, and risk-disclosure sources.
2. Executive risk assessment and changes from the prior period.
3. Business model and risk-to-financial transmission map.
4. Priority risk deep dives supported by operating and financial evidence.
5. Mitigation quality, absorption capacity, and residual exposure.
6. Reverse stress tests and scenario implications.
7. Monitoring dashboard with trigger, leading indicator, threshold, and affected account.
8. Disclosure limitations and conclusion tied to the decision.

## Boundaries

- Do not call the work an audit, review engagement, assurance opinion, or forensic conclusion.
- Do not infer fraud from an unusual balance or ratio.
- Do not give tax, legal, or personalised investment advice.
- Do not assume management-defined adjusted earnings are superior to reported earnings.
- Do not forecast unsupported precision from a short or inconsistent history.
