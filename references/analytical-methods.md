# Analytical methods

## Keep four layers separate

Write four working lines for every major point:

1. **Observation:** what the evidence directly shows.
2. **Mechanism:** why it may be happening.
3. **Implication:** what changes for the business decision.
4. **Action:** what the audience should do, if recommendations are in scope.

Mark forecasts and scenarios separately from all four.

## Compare before explaining

Choose a relevant baseline:

- prior period or pre-event level;
- budget, target, or business plan;
- peer, market, or industry benchmark;
- customer or operating segment;
- geography or channel;
- external consensus or dominant expectation;
- base-case scenario.

A difference is useful only if definitions and denominators match. Check whether it comes from composition, seasonality, data quality, timing, or a real change in behaviour.

## Build a driver tree

Use an accounting identity or operational model before writing a narrative. Examples:

- `revenue = customers x purchase frequency x average order value`
- `profit = revenue x gross margin - operating costs`
- `market = addressable users x adoption x usage x price`
- `productivity = output / labour or total input`
- `demand = segment population x incidence x usage intensity`

Turn changes into contributions when possible. Identify direct effects, indirect effects, lags, feedback loops, offsets, and overlap. Reconcile the parts to the total.

## Select fit-for-purpose modules

### Descriptive and exploratory analysis

Describe distribution, central tendency, dispersion, missingness, outliers, category frequency, and change over time. Explain why the pattern matters. Do not copy tables into prose.

### Segmentation

Create segments only when they reveal different behaviours, economics, risks, or actions. Define thresholds and test robustness. Compare within groups as well as across them to avoid aggregate effects.

### Correlation and diagnostic analysis

Report direction, magnitude, sample, method, and sensitivity to outliers or grouping. Compare relationships across meaningful subsets. Do not infer causality. Test plausible confounders or alternative explanations when data permits.

### Market and opportunity sizing

Reconcile top-down and bottom-up estimates. State the base year, currency, price basis, geography, included customers/products, adoption path, and sources for each driver. Use ranges when inputs do not support a precise point estimate.

### Value-chain and opportunity analysis

Map customer needs, business activities, enabling data and technology, partners, costs, risks, and revenue pools. Score opportunities by value, feasibility, strategic fit, dependencies, and time to impact. Adjust for overlap and shared enablers.

### Business case and value bridge

Use:

`incremental operating profit = revenue uplift x contribution margin + cost savings - recurring cost - implementation cost allocation`

Separate gross potential from realistically captured value. Model adoption, ramp time, cannibalisation, execution probability, shared costs, and cash timing. Show sensitivity to the assumptions that drive the answer.

### Financial statement analysis

Read [financial-statement-analysis.md](financial-statement-analysis.md). Start from disclosed risk, then trace the affected business activity into operating indicators, income-statement and balance-sheet accounts, cash flow, financial capacity, mitigation, and residual exposure. Use ratios only where they test a specific link in that chain.

### Forecast and scenario analysis

Begin with inherited momentum and measurable drivers. Separate temporary effects from structural trends and level changes from growth-rate changes. Use a base case plus material alternatives only when uncertainty could change the decision.

For each scenario specify trigger, assumptions, outcome, business effect, indicators, and invalidation. Add probabilities only when they help. If used, scenarios must be mutually exclusive and probabilities must total 100%.

### Maturity and capability analysis

Assess the capabilities the decision actually requires. Common dimensions include strategy, process, data, technology, governance, talent, incentives, funding, and ecosystem. Compare current state with required state and connect each gap to an outcome.

## Test the insight

A strong insight contains:

`evidence-backed pattern + mechanism + consequence for the decision`

Before keeping it, ask:

- Does it change a choice or priority?
- Is it more than a restatement of the chart?
- What is the strongest alternative explanation?
- Would it survive another definition or segment cut?
- Is the wording no stronger than the evidence?

## Turn analysis into recommendations

Use this chain:

`finding -> implication -> choice -> initiative -> owner -> prerequisite -> metric`

Prioritise by value, urgency, feasibility, reversibility, and dependency. Distinguish no-regret actions from contingent bets. Sequence recommendations by real dependencies, not by an arbitrary short-, medium-, and long-term template.

State what should not be prioritised when that makes the decision clearer.
