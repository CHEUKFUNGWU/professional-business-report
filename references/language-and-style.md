# Language and style

Apply this standard to English reports. Target the clarity and professional register expected in strong business writing, the coherence and grammatical control associated with IELTS Band 8, and the lexical precision expected of a reader comfortable with GRE-level vocabulary.

This is a writing target, not a claim that the report has received an IELTS score.

## Priority order

When goals conflict, use this order:

1. factual accuracy;
2. clarity of reasoning;
3. precision of wording;
4. concision;
5. sophistication of expression.

Never sacrifice the first four to sound advanced.

## Business register

Write for an educated manager, analyst, regulator, investor, or academic assessor. The prose should be formal without sounding ceremonial.

- State the conclusion early.
- Use evidence and quantities instead of intensifiers.
- Prefer active verbs and identifiable actors.
- Separate facts, estimates, interpretations, and recommendations.
- Calibrate confidence rather than sounding uniformly certain.
- Keep technical terms that professionals use, but define uncommon or company-specific measures.
- Avoid conversational filler, sales language, moralising, and rhetorical questions unless the format calls for them.

## IELTS Band 8 writing profile

### Task control

- Answer the exact business question and fulfil every required section.
- Develop each major claim with relevant evidence and analysis.
- Remove technically correct material that does not serve the purpose.
- Maintain a consistent audience, scope, and time horizon.

### Coherence and cohesion

- Give each paragraph one controlling idea.
- Arrange paragraphs so each answers the question created by the previous one.
- Use topic sentences that make claims rather than announce topics.
- Refer back with precise nouns instead of vague terms such as "this" or "these factors."
- Use transitions only when the logical relationship is not already clear.
- Avoid mechanical sequencing such as "firstly," "secondly," and "moreover" in every paragraph.

### Grammatical range and accuracy

- Maintain near-error-free grammar across the report.
- Vary simple, compound, and complex sentences according to the logic.
- Use subordination to express cause, contrast, condition, concession, and consequence.
- Keep modifiers close to what they modify.
- Maintain parallel structure in genuine lists.
- Control articles, agreement, tense, prepositions, punctuation, and countable nouns.
- Break a sentence when its clauses force the reader to reread it.

Complex grammar should clarify relationships. It should not create long sentences for their own sake.

## GRE-level lexical precision

Use advanced vocabulary when it captures a specific analytical distinction. Useful business terms include:

- **Material:** large enough to affect the decision or statements.
- **Attributable:** reasonably assigned to a stated driver.
- **Corroborate:** support a claim with independent evidence.
- **Reconcile:** explain and resolve a difference between measures.
- **Disaggregate:** separate a total into meaningful components.
- **Deteriorate:** become measurably worse.
- **Mitigate:** reduce probability or impact without necessarily removing the risk.
- **Exacerbate:** make an existing problem worse.
- **Constrain:** limit an outcome or available choice.
- **Contingent:** dependent on a specified condition.
- **Disproportionate:** larger or smaller than the relevant base would suggest.
- **Transitory:** temporary under a stated mechanism and horizon.
- **Structural:** caused by persistent features rather than a short cycle.
- **Resilient:** able to absorb a specified shock while maintaining defined functions.
- **Accretive or dilutive:** increasing or decreasing a clearly named per-unit financial measure.
- **Prudent:** justified by the evidence, risk, and decision context.

Do not insert these words merely to make the prose sound advanced. The sentence must supply the object, mechanism, or comparison that makes the word meaningful.

Avoid obscure or literary synonyms when common business language is more exact. Prefer "use" to "utilise," "help" to "facilitate," and "worsen" to "exacerbate" unless the advanced term expresses the intended nuance.

## Strong analytical verbs

Choose verbs that describe what the evidence does:

- data **show**, **indicate**, **corroborate**, **contradict**, or **do not establish**;
- a driver **accounts for**, **contributes to**, **offsets**, **amplifies**, or **constrains** a change;
- a result **exceeds**, **lags**, **converges with**, **diverges from**, or **remains within** a benchmark;
- a risk **transmits through**, **crystallises in**, **is absorbed by**, or **remains after** mitigation;
- management **allocated**, **deferred**, **repriced**, **hedged**, **refinanced**, or **discontinued** an activity.

Avoid weak constructions such as "there was an increase in revenue." Write "revenue increased 8%." Name the driver when evidence supports it.

## Evidential calibration

Match language to the evidence:

| Evidence strength | Suitable language |
|---|---|
| Direct, verified observation | shows, records, reports, demonstrates within the sample |
| Reproducible association | is associated with, coincides with, varies with |
| Supported interpretation | suggests, indicates, is consistent with |
| Forecast or model output | estimates, projects, forecasts, implies under the stated assumptions |
| Plausible but weak explanation | may reflect, could arise from, remains a possible explanation |

Use "causes," "drives," or "results in" only when the research design or mechanism supports causality. Use "will" only for contractual, definitional, or otherwise highly certain outcomes. Prefer "is expected to" or "would" for forecasts and scenarios.

## Sentence and paragraph rhythm

Use short sentences for conclusions and longer sentences for genuine qualification. Most sentences should carry one principal claim. A paragraph normally contains:

1. the claim;
2. the strongest evidence;
3. the mechanism or comparison;
4. the qualification or implication.

Do not force every paragraph into this sequence. Repetition makes professional writing sound automated.

## Replace vague business language

Replace phrases such as:

- "strong performance" with the metric, period, and benchmark;
- "significant growth" with the measured change;
- "various challenges" with the named constraints;
- "market dynamics" with the actual price, volume, cost, regulation, or competitor change;
- "strategic initiative" with the action taken;
- "unlock value" with the revenue, cost, cash, risk, or capability effect;
- "industry-leading" with the peer measure that supports the claim;
- "robust" with the stress, sample, or test the result survived.

## Final language audit

Before delivery, check:

1. Does every paragraph advance the argument?
2. Does each advanced word express a distinction that a simpler word would lose?
3. Are grammar and punctuation controlled throughout, including tables and headings?
4. Are sentence structures varied without becoming dense?
5. Are transitions logical rather than formulaic?
6. Are claims calibrated to evidence?
7. Could an informed reader understand every sentence on the first reading?
8. Does the report sound like a competent analyst rather than a thesaurus or marketing brochure?

## Chinese reports

When the user requests Chinese, preserve the same analytical standard through concise professional Chinese. Do not translate advanced English vocabulary literally. Use established Chinese financial and business terminology, retain English abbreviations only when common in the field, and define them once.
